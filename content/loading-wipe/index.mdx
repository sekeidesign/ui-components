Loading states almost always leave by fading. A fade is a dissolve of the whole
plane at once, which is why it reads as the spinner giving up rather than the
page arriving. This one leaves by being swept off — a single edge travelling
across the surface, with the content already sitting behind it.

## Revealing, not compositing

The canvas writes premultiplied alpha and sits over the page rather than under
it. Where the sweep has passed, the fragment's alpha is zero and the real DOM
underneath shows through — no screenshot, no cross-fade between two copies of
the same layout. The skeleton here was never hidden; it was covered.

That constraint shapes everything else. There is no "after" state to animate
towards, so the edge is the entire effect, and it has to be worth looking at.

## The edge

`dot(uv, dir) + bias` gives a signed distance from a line at any angle,
normalised over the unit square so the sweep runs corner to corner whatever the
aspect ratio — at 45° it reduces to `(uv.x + uv.y) / 2`. A band of fbm noise
bends that line as it travels, and a four-stop ramp fills the space behind it,
its coordinate smeared by a second, independent noise field so the colours
marble into each other instead of striping.

## What has to be pixels

The edge is turbulent, feathered and grainy, and that shape exists only as
alpha inside the shader. CSS masks take images and gradients, not live GPU
output — so anything the sweep is supposed to carry off has to be *in* the
shader, or it leaves on a visibly different edge from the surface it was
sitting on. A hard-edged polygon travelling through a soft, noisy one is
obvious immediately.

That splits the layers by what each one needs. The surface is a bitmap the
fragment samples as its base colour, so one alpha field governs it. The page
underneath — the skeleton — is real DOM and never rasterised; it is being
revealed, not removed. And the loader's text is real DOM too, because it
leaves by fading and rising rather than by being swept, and anything that
doesn't need the sweep's alpha has no reason to stop being an element.

Which leaves a rule worth keeping: rasterise only what is about to disappear.

## Grain

Grain offsets the edge distance rather than the finished alpha. Offsetting
alpha punches holes through the solid surface as well; offsetting the distance
only breaks up the edge as it passes, which is the part that should look eaten.

The grain needed its own hash. The fract-multiply hash driving the fbm is fine
there — its inputs are small — but fed the large integer coordinates a
per-pixel grain needs, its fractional parts cycle with a short period and f32
precision collapses. Measured over 40,000 cells it produced 971 distinct
values, and the eye finds that instantly: hard diagonal lattices right across
the sweep. A 32-bit bit-mixing hash gives 39,204 instead.

## Cost

A WebGPU device costs while it exists, not while it animates, and this sits in
a feed. So nothing initialises until you ask it to: the surface bitmap is shown
directly at first, and the device is created on the first play or scrub, then
destroyed when the card scrolls away.

That same bitmap is what the shader samples once a device does exist. Sharing
the pixels rather than generating the grain twice is what makes the handover
invisible — a shader-side grain and a canvas-side grain are the same grain
statistically, but swapping between them makes the whole plane change texture
at once.

Where WebGPU is missing the same geometry runs as a `clip-path` polygon — the
unit square clipped by the sweep's half-plane. Same angle, same duration, hard
edge, no ribbon.

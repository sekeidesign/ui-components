When we mapped job stories for the quarter, the same ask kept coming back
in different shapes: send Tato's output to the tools I already work in,
without more effort on my part. A skill that surfaces open risks is
valuable, but only if someone runs it. The moment a workflow needs active
effort to trigger, it stops happening.

## Approach

I learned in our research process that the highest value work is strategic. 
I also learned that this type of work is often bogged down by the clerical work: 
extracting meeting outputs, moving data from one system to another, generating recurring outputs, etc.

A routine is three choices: a skill or report that works well, a trigger,
and a destination. Pick your weekly risk summary, schedule it for Monday
morning, send it to the steering committee's inbox. From then on it just
arrives.

<Figure
	src="/casestudies/automation-routines/automations-empty-state.webp"
	alt="The automations empty state, with templates for a weekly status report, a Monday prep digest, and a team wins & recognition digest"
	caption="Starting from a template instead of a blank routine"
/>

To make that first routine easier to reach for, empty projects ship with a
few ready-made templates — a weekly status report, a Monday morning
prep digest, a team wins round-up — so the first one a user creates is a
tweak, not a blank page.

### Meeting users where they work

The destination is what makes a routine worth having. Our users spend
most of their day in Teams and email, rather than in Tato, so we built integrations with
both and made them first-class outputs. A routine can post to a Teams
channel, a private Teams message, an inbox, or Tato's own chat, which
means Tato does the work where people actually need it instead of waiting
to be checked.

### Time and event based triggers

Most routines run on a schedule — daily, weekly, or monthly, optionally
scoped to weekdays. But some workflows aren't calendar-shaped, they're
tied to an event. So a routine can also trigger off a meeting, firing the
moment one is created or right after it wraps, narrowed down with filters
on the meeting name, the organizer, or the attendees.

<Figure
	src="/casestudies/automation-routines/meeting-trigger-flow.webp"

A daily scrum agenda routine is a good example of the pattern: it fires
right after the standup ends, pulls open risks and action items straight
from the project's RAID log, and posts the summary to both a Teams
channel and Tato's own chat.

<Figure
	src="/casestudies/automation-routines/daily-scrum-output.webp"

### Keeping v1 small

We cut aggressively. Multi-step routines and conditional logic went to v2,
and even the meeting trigger above shipped after the first cut. The
definition of done was one sentence: a user can schedule a skill to run
weekly and the output lands in an inbox. Everything else could wait until
that worked.

## Outcome

Routines turned Tato's outputs from something you fetch into something that
arrives. A risk summary before the steering committee, a decision log at
the end of the week, without anyone remembering to ask. It also became the
base other features build on: report scheduling and output routing were
unified on top of routines soon after.

Routines were the biggest lever on an objective I own: make Tato's AI
touchpoints so frictionless that using them becomes instinctive instead of
a context switch, measured by doubling the number of times Tato gets
invoked. Every routine that runs is an invocation nobody had to remember.
